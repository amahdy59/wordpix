## 2. Read & Understand

### Degree, Debt, and Opportunity

For generations, university has been presented as a major path to professional opportunity. A degree can provide specialist knowledge, access to careers that require formal qualifications, professional networks, and the chance to study a subject deeply. In many labour markets, graduates also earn more on average than people without a degree.

But “worth it” is not a simple question. Tuition can be expensive, especially where students take out large loans. A degree also has an opportunity cost: years spent studying are years in which a person might otherwise work, build a business, or complete vocational training.

A 2024 Pew Research Center analysis in the United States found mixed public views. Only 22% of adults said a four-year degree was worth the cost even if loans were required, while 47% said it was worth the cost only if a student did not need loans. At the same time, economic data in the report continued to show important differences in outcomes between young adults with and without bachelor’s degrees.

The value also varies by subject, institution, country, and career. Medicine and engineering do not lead to the same opportunities as every other degree, and some high-paying careers can be entered through apprenticeships, technical education, certifications, or experience.

University may also offer benefits that are difficult to measure in salary alone: independence, friendships, exposure to new ideas, research experience, and personal development.

The answer also changes over time. A degree can have value beyond the first salary after graduation, while debt or lost earnings during study can matter immediately. Students therefore need to compare both short-term costs and long-term possibilities rather than treating “university” as one identical product for everyone.

So asking whether university is “worth it” may be too broad. A better question is: **Worth it for whom, for which goal, at what price, and compared with which alternative?** A degree can open doors, but it is no longer sensible to assume that every degree automatically pays off in the same way.

### In Short

A university **degree** can **open doors** and improve **career prospects**, but high **tuition** and student **debt** change the calculation. Learners should compare university with alternatives such as **vocational training** and ask whether the investment is likely to **pay off**.

## 4. Conversation Toolkit

### Supporting a Claim Without Overgeneralising

**The evidence suggests that...**

> The evidence suggests that degrees still provide economic benefits on average.

**That tends to be true when...**

> That tends to be true when the cost is reasonable and the degree leads to strong opportunities.

**We should also consider...**

> We should also consider vocational alternatives.

**It doesn’t follow that...**

> Graduates earn more on average, but it doesn’t follow that every degree is a good investment.

## 6. Discussion

### 1. Purpose

Should university mainly prepare people for jobs, or serve broader goals?

### 2. Debt

At what point does education debt become unreasonable?

### 3. Alternatives

Which careers are better served by vocational training than university?

### 4. Employers

Should companies require degrees for jobs that do not clearly need one?

### 5. Status

Do some people choose university partly because of social pressure?

### 6. Challenge

If graduates earn more on average, is that enough evidence to say university causes the difference?
