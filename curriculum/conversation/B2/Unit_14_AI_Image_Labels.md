## 2. Read & Understand

### When Seeing Is No Longer Believing

For much of modern history, photographs have carried a special kind of authority. People know that photos can be staged or edited, but an image still often feels like evidence that something happened. Generative AI is making that assumption harder to maintain.

AI systems can now create realistic people, places, and events that never existed. They can also alter genuine images in ways that are difficult to detect. This can be harmless in art, advertising, or entertainment, but the same technology can be used for impersonation, fraud, or misleading public information.

One response is labelling. From August 2026, European Union AI Act transparency requirements apply to certain AI-generated or manipulated content. Deepfakes must be disclosed, and providers must add machine-readable marks to synthetic content in covered cases. The aim is to reduce deception and help people understand what they are seeing.

But where should the line be drawn? Almost every professional photo may be adjusted for brightness, colour, or framing. Requiring a warning for every small edit could make labels meaningless. On the other hand, only labelling completely invented images may miss important manipulations, such as changing a person’s body, adding someone to a scene, or removing something that changes the meaning.

The form of the label matters as well. A tiny technical marker that ordinary viewers never notice may help automated systems but do little for human understanding. A large warning on harmless creative content may have the opposite problem: it can attract attention without explaining what was actually changed. Useful transparency should communicate the kind and significance of the alteration, not merely announce that “AI was used.”

Labels also do not solve every problem. People may ignore them, they may disappear when content is copied, and bad actors may deliberately remove them. Technical detection and media literacy are still necessary.

The deeper issue is authenticity. Audiences need enough information to judge whether an image represents reality, artistic creation, or deliberate manipulation. The challenge is creating transparency without treating ordinary creative editing as deception.

### In Short

AI can create highly realistic but **altered** content, including a **deepfake** that may be **passed off as** real. Clear **disclosure** and **transparency** can protect **authenticity**, but labels need to distinguish meaningful manipulation from ordinary editing.

## 4. Conversation Toolkit

### Assigning Responsibility

**It should be the responsibility of... to...**

> It should be the responsibility of platforms to preserve labels when content is shared.

**The creator has a duty to...**

> The creator has a duty to disclose major manipulation.

**A label is justified when...**

> A label is justified when an edit changes what viewers believe happened.

**The difficult case is...**

> The difficult case is artistic editing that looks realistic but is not intended as news.
