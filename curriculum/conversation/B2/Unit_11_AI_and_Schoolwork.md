## 2. Read & Understand

### Tutor, Tool, or Shortcut?

Generative AI can explain a difficult idea, suggest examples, translate a passage, create practice questions, or give feedback on a first draft. Used carefully, it can act like a tutor that is available at any time. It may also help students who need extra explanations or who are learning in a second language.

The problem is that the same tool can produce an entire assignment in seconds. If a student asks AI to write an essay and then hands it in as personal work, the teacher can no longer see what the student actually knows. This creates an academic-integrity problem and can turn AI from a learning tool into a shortcut.

There is another issue: AI answers are not automatically reliable. A system can produce false information, weak reasoning, or invented sources while sounding confident. Students therefore need to verify important claims rather than take an answer at face value.

UNESCO’s guidance on generative AI in education takes a human-centred approach. It encourages education systems to think about privacy, age, ethics, and the development of human skills rather than treating AI as either completely forbidden or automatically beneficial. UNESCO also stresses the importance of AI literacy for students and teachers.

This suggests that the most useful classroom question may not be “Can students use AI?” but “What kind of use actually supports learning?” Asking AI to explain why an equation is wrong may strengthen understanding. Asking it to complete the whole assignment may remove the learning process itself.

Schools therefore face a difficult policy choice. Rules need to protect genuine student work without pretending that a widely available technology does not exist. The goal is not simply to catch misuse; it is to teach students how to use powerful tools without allowing those tools to do all the thinking for them.

### In Short

AI can provide **feedback**, help students produce a **draft**, and support learning, but it can also become a **shortcut** that threatens **academic integrity**. Students need to **verify** information and avoid **taking answers at face value** when AI sounds confident.

## 4. Conversation Toolkit

### Giving a Counterargument

**That’s a fair point, but...**

> That’s a fair point, but students still need to learn how to check AI output.

**One argument against that is...**

> One argument against that is that bans are difficult to enforce.

**Even if..., it doesn’t necessarily mean...**

> Even if AI saves time, it doesn’t necessarily mean students learn more.

**The key distinction is between...**

> The key distinction is between receiving help and replacing your own work.
