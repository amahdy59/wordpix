## 2. Read & Understand

### When Easier Is Not Always Better

Modern life is full of services designed to remove effort. Food can arrive at the door, a taxi can appear after two taps, music starts instantly, bills can be paid automatically, and an algorithm can suggest what to watch next.

Convenience saves time and can make important services easier to access. Online markets give people more choice, and digital tools can help people who have limited mobility, live far from shops, or have very busy schedules. OECD work on digital consumer markets highlights both greater convenience and greater choice as major benefits of digitalisation.

But every convenience changes behaviour. If food delivery is always available, a person may cook less. If maps always show the route, people may stop learning their city. If an app remembers every password or decision, we may rely on it without noticing how dependent we have become.

Convenience can also hide costs. A “free” service may use personal data. One-click buying can make spending easier before people have time to think. Recommendation systems can save time but also reduce the chance of discovering something outside our usual interests.

None of this means convenience is bad. Few people want to return to waiting in long lines just because waiting builds patience. The important question is what we gain and what we quietly give up.

Useful convenience removes unnecessary effort. Unhelpful convenience may remove choices, skills, movement, privacy, or moments of reflection that actually matter.

The challenge is noticing the difference before a helpful tool becomes something we cannot imagine living without.

### In Short

Convenience can **save time** and put services **at your fingertips**, but it can also increase **dependence** when people **rely on** technology for every small task. We may even **take for granted** tools that quietly change our habits, choices, and privacy.

## 4. Conversation Toolkit

### Summarising and Defending a View

**Overall, I think...**

> Overall, I think convenience improves life.

**My main reason is...**

> My main reason is that it saves time.

**However, we should remember...**

> However, we should remember that convenience can have hidden costs.

**So, for me...**

> So, for me, the best tools are the ones I can choose not to use.

## 7. Speaking Challenge

### The Convenience Audit

Choose five conveniences from daily life—such as delivery apps, digital payments, maps, streaming, AI assistants, ride-hailing, or automatic subscriptions. For each one, identify **what it saves**, **what it may cost**, and whether you would keep it. Finish by ranking the five from most valuable to least valuable.
