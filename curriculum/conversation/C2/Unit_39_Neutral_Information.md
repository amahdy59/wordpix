## 2. Read & Understand

### Facts Do Not Arrive Already Edited

Imagine that a company announces 1,000 new jobs while closing a smaller division and eliminating 200 positions. One headline says, “Company Creates 1,000 Jobs.” Another says, “Company Cuts 200 Roles During Restructuring.” Both statements can be factually correct. Yet they direct attention toward different parts of the same event.

This illustrates **framing**: the choices through which information is selected, organised, emphasised, and placed in context. Framing does not automatically mean dishonesty. Every report must decide what belongs in the first paragraph, which numbers need comparison, whose voices are relevant, and how much background the audience needs. Infinite context is impossible.

UNESCO's media and information literacy framework explicitly notes that information, knowledge, and messages are not always value-neutral or independent of bias, and argues that these influences should be made transparent and understandable. That does not imply that every claim is equally reliable. Evidence can still be checked, sources can be evaluated, numbers can be verified, and false statements can be distinguished from well-supported ones.

This distinction is crucial. If we conclude that perfect neutrality is impossible and therefore all information is “just opinion,” we erase meaningful differences in method. A carefully sourced report that corrects errors, separates news from commentary, verifies evidence, and represents relevant uncertainty is not equivalent to a fabricated post simply because both involve selection.

**Impartiality** is one professional ideal used by some news organisations. It usually means trying not to adopt a particular side's viewpoint as the report's own, while still making evidence-based judgments. The Reuters Institute's 2026 Digital News Report surveyed people across 48 markets and found that 45% said they preferred news from sources that do not have a particular point of view, compared with 22% who preferred sources that share their point of view and 15% who preferred sources that challenge it. The researchers stress that these are self-reported preferences, not direct measures of actual news behaviour, and that preferences vary by country.

Even an impartiality standard raises difficult questions. If evidence strongly supports one claim over another, presenting two positions as equally credible can create **false balance**. Conversely, a reporter who believes one side has stronger evidence must still explain how that judgment was reached rather than silently treating it as self-evident.

Language also carries associations. Calling a proposal “reform,” “rollback,” “investment,” or “cost” can frame the same action differently. Images, statistics, expert selection, and omission can do the same.

Complete neutrality may therefore be less useful as a promise than **procedural fairness**: transparent sourcing, consistent standards, proportionate attention to evidence, correction of mistakes, and clarity about what is fact, inference, and opinion.

Information may never arrive without a frame. The more practical question is whether we can see the frame—and whether the evidence still holds when we look through a different one.

### In Short

**Framing** is unavoidable whenever information is selected and organised, but that does not make all claims equally subjective. **Impartiality** can function as a method even if perfect neutrality is difficult, while **false balance** can occur when unequal evidence is presented as equal. **Procedural fairness** and transparent sourcing help audiences distinguish evidence from interpretation.

## 4. Conversation Toolkit

### Evaluating framing without collapsing fact into opinion

**We should separate the factual claim from the framing choice.**

> We should separate the factual claim about 1,000 hires from the framing choice about which number leads the story.

**The existence of perspective does not imply that...**

> The existence of perspective does not imply that every account is equally accurate.

**A stronger test would be whether...**

> A stronger test would be whether the conclusion holds up under different reasonable framings.

**Impartiality here should mean..., not...**

> Impartiality here should mean consistent evidentiary standards, not pretending all claims have equal support.
