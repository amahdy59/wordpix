## 2. Read & Understand

### When “Real” Becomes a Feature

For most of history, producing a convincing photograph, recording, illustration, or written document required a chain of human actions that left clues about origin. Generative AI changes that environment. Synthetic images, voices, videos, and text can now be created quickly and at scale, sometimes closely resembling material produced or recorded by people.

That does not make the concept of **authenticity** disappear, but it makes it more complicated. Authenticity can mean at least three things. A work can be authentic because its _origin_ is what it claims to be: a photograph really came from a particular camera at a particular event. It can be authentic because it genuinely expresses the creator's intentions or identity. Or it can be authentic in the looser sense of feeling sincere, even if the material is partly constructed.

AI puts pressure on all three meanings. A synthetic voice can sound emotionally sincere without having emotions. A human artist can use AI extensively while still making deliberate creative choices. A real photograph can be misleading if it is taken out of context, while an obviously artificial image can communicate a truthful idea.

This is why **provenance**—information about where content came from and how it was changed—is becoming increasingly important. Technical systems can attach metadata or machine-readable signals to some content, while platforms and publishers can use visible labels. In the European Union, transparency obligations under the AI Act began applying on 2 August 2026 in specified cases, including labelling certain deepfakes and AI-generated or manipulated text on matters of public interest when it lacks qualifying human review and editorial responsibility. These rules are one regulatory approach, not a universal global standard.

Labels, however, cannot solve the entire problem. Metadata can be removed, content can be copied across platforms, and users may ignore warnings. **Verification** therefore becomes a social process as well as a technical one: checking sources, comparing evidence, and understanding how content reached us.

Paradoxically, an abundance of synthetic material could increase the value of things whose origin can be trusted. Live performances, signed physical objects, verified recordings, direct human interaction, and transparent creative processes may acquire a kind of **scarcity value**. “Made by a person” could become a feature people are willing to seek out, much as handmade products can remain valuable in an industrial economy.

But authenticity could also become less connected to origin. Younger audiences may care more about whether content is useful, entertaining, or emotionally meaningful than whether every element was human-made. Hybrid works could make the binary categories “AI” and “human” increasingly unhelpful.

The apparent contradiction is therefore real: AI can make authenticity harder to establish while making trustworthy origin more valuable. The future meaning of “authentic” may depend not on excluding technology, but on being clearer about what exactly we want authenticated.

### In Short

AI makes **authenticity** harder to define because origin, sincerity, and truth are not the same. **Provenance** and **verification** can help users **trace content back** to its source, while trustworthy human origin may gain **scarcity value**. At the same time, hybrid creation can **blur the line** between “human” and “AI.”

## 4. Conversation Toolkit

### Resolving apparent contradictions and defining authenticity

**There are at least two senses of...**

> There are at least two senses of authenticity: verified origin and sincere expression.

**The apparent contradiction disappears if...**

> The apparent contradiction disappears if authenticity becomes more valuable precisely because it is harder to establish.

**That would authenticate X, but not necessarily Y.**

> That would authenticate the source, but not necessarily the truth of the message.

**The binary breaks down when...**

> The human-versus-AI binary breaks down when creators use AI as one component of a larger process.
