## 2. Read & Understand

### A Better Tool Is Not Automatically a Better World

Technology is one of the strongest forces behind long-run improvements in human capability. Vaccines, sanitation systems, antibiotics, agricultural machinery, electricity, refrigeration, communications networks, and countless other innovations have changed what people can survive, produce, learn, and coordinate.

The historical evidence is substantial. Our World in Data notes that technological change underpins developments across health, agriculture, energy, and global development. Its life-expectancy data show global life expectancy rising from about 32 years in 1900 to 71 years in 2021, alongside improvements in medicine, public health, nutrition, sanitation, living standards, and other factors. Literacy has also expanded dramatically over the long run. These changes cannot be attributed to technology alone—institutions, public policy, economic development, education, and social organisation all matter—but many of the improvements would have been impossible without new tools and knowledge.

The word **progress**, however, contains a value judgment. A faster machine is a technological improvement if speed is the objective. Whether it represents human progress depends on what the speed is used for, who benefits, what costs accompany it, and what other values are affected.

Consider a technology that doubles productivity but concentrates nearly all gains among a small group. It represents technical progress and economic output, yet its effect on broader well-being is ambiguous. A platform that makes communication effortless may strengthen distant relationships while increasing distraction, surveillance, or misinformation. A medical technology may save lives but remain inaccessible to most of the people who need it. Technologies can expand **capability** without distributing that capability equally.

Innovation can also create **externalities**—costs or benefits experienced by people who did not choose the technology directly. Fossil-fuel technologies helped power industrial development while contributing to pollution and greenhouse-gas emissions. Antibiotics transformed medicine while overuse contributed to antimicrobial resistance. Social platforms enable mass communication while creating new forms of attention competition and information risk. These examples do not mean the technologies were simply “good” or “bad.” They show that benefits and harms can unfold at different times and scales.

Another complication is adaptation. When a technology solves one constraint, expectations change. Faster communication does not necessarily create more free time; organisations may simply expect faster responses. Greater productivity can increase consumption rather than leisure. Convenience can remove effort that was unpleasant, but sometimes also remove skills or social contact that people later value.

Human progress therefore requires a broader **scorecard**. Health, freedom, knowledge, security, opportunity, environmental quality, social connection, distribution, and meaning may move in different directions. No single indicator captures all of them.

The final question of this course is not whether technology matters. It clearly does. It is whether we can distinguish a new capability from a better human outcome—and design institutions, norms, and choices that convert more of the first into the second.

### In Short

**Technological progress** expands human **capability**, but human progress depends on how benefits and harms are distributed. Innovations can create **externalities**, produce difficult **trade-offs**, and have **unintended consequences**. A broader **scorecard** asks not only what technology can do, but whether it improves outcomes people actually value.

## 4. Conversation Toolkit

### Full-course synthesis and evaluating progress across competing dimensions

**We need a broader scorecard than...**

> We need a broader scorecard than productivity alone.

**That is technical progress, but whether it is human progress depends on...**

> That is technical progress, but whether it is human progress depends on distribution, side effects, and the values we prioritise.

**The first-order benefit is..., while a second-order effect may be...**

> The first-order benefit is faster communication, while a second-order effect may be an expectation of constant availability.

**The technology is a means to an end; the end is...**

> The technology is a means to an end; the end is a life people have reason to value.
