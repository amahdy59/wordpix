3. What should count as “merit”: talent, effort, results, contribution, or something else?

## 2. Read & Understand

### The Problem Hidden Inside “Merit”

Meritocracy sounds straightforward: positions and rewards should go to people according to ability and achievement rather than family background, wealth, or social connections. The appeal is obvious. If two people compete for the same opportunity, many of us instinctively want the outcome to reflect what they can do rather than who their parents are.

The difficulty begins with the word _merit_. Is merit natural talent? Hard work? Qualifications? Measurable results? The value someone creates for other people? These are not interchangeable. A gifted person who makes little effort and a less gifted person who works relentlessly may deserve recognition for different reasons. Even apparently objective measures, such as test scores or professional experience, are shaped by earlier opportunities.

This matters because people do not begin from the same **starting point**. An OECD analysis published in 2025 estimated that, across the OECD countries it examined, more than a quarter of inequality in household market income could be attributed to circumstances beyond an individual's control, such as parental socio-economic background, sex, and country of birth. Parental background was a particularly important factor. These estimates vary considerably between countries, and the analysis describes statistical patterns rather than proving that any one circumstance directly causes a particular person's outcome.

A defender of meritocracy might reply that unequal starting conditions do not make merit meaningless. Education, fair recruitment, transparent rules, and access to training can **level the playing field** without pretending that every difference can disappear. On this view, the aim is not perfect equality of outcomes but a system in which inherited advantage matters less and achievement matters more.

Critics raise a deeper objection. They argue that success itself can create advantages that are then passed to the next generation. A person who earns wealth through genuine achievement may naturally spend it on better housing, education, networks, and opportunities for their children. The next generation therefore starts from a different position. A system can reward merit at one stage and reproduce unequal opportunity at the next.

There is also a psychological problem. If society describes success entirely as deserved, successful people may underestimate the role of luck, support, or favourable circumstances. Those who struggle may be treated as if their situation reflects a personal failure even when structural barriers matter.

Even the attempt to correct unequal opportunity can create new disputes about fairness. Giving extra support to someone who faced greater barriers may improve substantive opportunity, while critics may see different treatment as inconsistent with equal rules. The disagreement often reflects two different ideas of fairness: treating everyone identically at the point of competition, or compensating for relevant differences that existed before it.

Perhaps the most useful question is not whether a perfectly meritocratic society is possible. It may be whether institutions can distinguish **earned advantage** from **inherited advantage** well enough to make opportunities meaningfully fairer—without denying either individual effort or the reality of unequal circumstances.

### In Short

A **meritocracy** tries to reward achievement, but people begin from unequal **starting points** and may face **structural barriers**. Policies and institutions can try to **level the playing field**, yet **inherited advantage** can persist across generations, making the boundary between what is earned and what is inherited difficult to draw.

## 4. Conversation Toolkit

### Questioning premises and defining contested concepts

**That depends on what we mean by...**

> That depends on what we mean by “merit.”

**The premise seems to assume that...**

> The premise seems to assume that achievement can be separated cleanly from opportunity.

**Even if we accept that..., it does not necessarily follow that...**

> Even if we accept that effort should be rewarded, it does not necessarily follow that every unequal outcome is fair.

**A useful distinction is between... and...**

> A useful distinction is between earned advantage and inherited advantage.

## 6. Discussion

### 1. Definition

Which matters more when judging merit: effort, ability, results, or contribution?

### 2. Starting Conditions

How unequal can starting conditions be before a competition stops feeling fair?

### 3. Luck

Should people receive less credit for success if luck played an important role?

### 4. Inheritance

Is it fair for parents to pass advantages to their children if they earned those advantages themselves?

### 5. Measurement

Can tests, interviews, and qualifications ever measure merit without also measuring opportunity?

### 6. Premise

Is “perfect meritocracy” a useful goal, or is it an impossible standard that distracts from improving fairness?
