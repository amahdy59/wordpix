## 2. Read & Understand

### Decisions With Stakeholders Who Cannot Speak

Every generation inherits a world it did not choose. Roads, debts, institutions, technologies, languages, ecosystems, scientific knowledge, pollution, and cultural norms all arrive from the past. Many decisions we make today will similarly shape people who are not yet alive and therefore cannot vote, negotiate, protest, buy shares, or leave a review.

This creates a distinctive ethical problem. Normally, affected people can express preferences or claim rights. **Future generations** cannot participate directly. We also do not know exactly who they will be, how many there will be, or what technologies and values they will have. Yet uncertainty does not make all future effects irrelevant. Some consequences—long-lived waste, species extinction, infrastructure choices, depleted resources, or persistent public debt—can constrain options for decades or centuries.

In 2024, United Nations member states adopted a Declaration on Future Generations as part of the Pact for the Future. The declaration describes future generations as generations that do not yet exist and recognises that present decisions, actions, and inactions can have an “intergenerational multiplier effect.” It commits states politically to safeguard future needs and interests across areas such as sustainable development, peace, human rights, and decision-making. It is not the same thing as establishing a universal enforceable legal right for every future person, and national legal approaches vary.

One ethical principle is **stewardship**: the present generation temporarily controls resources and institutions that it did not create entirely for itself and should pass them on in a condition that leaves reasonable possibilities open. Another is **intergenerational justice**, which asks how benefits and burdens should be distributed across people separated by time.

But there is a serious counterargument. Present people also have urgent needs. Spending enormous resources to reduce a distant, uncertain risk may mean spending less on poverty, healthcare, education, or safety today. Future societies may also be wealthier or possess technologies that make current sacrifices unnecessary. Giving unlimited priority to the distant future could therefore be as unfair as ignoring it.

Economists sometimes represent this tension through **discounting**, giving future costs and benefits less weight than immediate ones. Some discounting reflects practical factors such as uncertainty or alternative uses of resources; ethical debates arise over how strongly the well-being of future people should be discounted merely because it occurs later.

The challenge becomes especially difficult when choices are **irreversible**. If future people dislike an institution, they may change it. If they inherit extinct species or permanent contamination, the option itself has disappeared.

Perhaps our strongest obligations arise not from predicting exactly what future people will want, but from preserving their capacity to choose. On that view, the task is not to design their world for them. It is to avoid **locking in** avoidable harms or unnecessarily closing possibilities before they have any voice in the matter.

### In Short

**Intergenerational justice** asks how present choices should account for people who do not yet exist. **Stewardship** emphasises passing on resources and institutions responsibly, while **discounting** reflects disputes over how much weight to give future costs and benefits. The strongest concern often arises when today's actions **lock in** **irreversible** consequences.

## 4. Conversation Toolkit

### Reasoning across time and representing absent stakeholders

**The moral difficulty arises because...**

> The moral difficulty arises because future people are affected but cannot participate in the decision.

**Uncertainty weakens our ability to predict..., but it does not eliminate...**

> Uncertainty weakens our ability to predict future preferences, but it does not eliminate foreseeable risks.

**I would give greater weight to cases where...**

> I would give greater weight to cases where the harm is irreversible and alternatives remain available today.

**The obligation may be to preserve options rather than to...**

> The obligation may be to preserve options rather than to decide exactly how future people should live.
