# General Conversation Curriculum — Curriculum Guide

## Overview

This curriculum is a 40-unit English conversation pathway from **B1 to C2**. It is designed for adult and young-adult learners who want to improve spoken English through substantive discussion rather than isolated grammar drills.

The course progresses through four communication stages:

| Level  | Core Stage   | Learner Development                                                                                                                 |
| ------ | ------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **B1** | **EXPRESS**  | State opinions, give reasons and examples, compare options, agree and disagree politely, and reach simple decisions.                |
| **B2** | **DEFEND**   | Support claims, respond to counterarguments, speculate, make concessions, weigh alternatives, and persuade.                         |
| **C1** | **ANALYSE**  | Qualify claims, identify assumptions, evaluate evidence, reframe issues, reconcile competing interests, and synthesise viewpoints.  |
| **C2** | **NAVIGATE** | Define contested concepts, question premises, manage ambiguity, examine second-order consequences, and construct nuanced syntheses. |

The curriculum is **CEFR-informed**, but completing 40 lessons alone should not be presented as sufficient to move a learner automatically from B1 to C2. The units provide a structured conversation and language-development pathway that can sit inside a wider programme of listening, reading, writing, grammar, pronunciation, and extensive speaking practice.

## Locked Lesson Structure

Every learner-facing unit uses the same compact seven-part architecture.

### 1. Big Question & Warm-up

One central discussion question, three warm-up prompts, and a quick initial vote. The aim is to activate prior knowledge and make learners commit to an initial position before reading.

### 2. Read & Understand

An original, source-synthesised reading appropriate to the CEFR level. The article introduces more than one perspective and avoids turning a complex issue into a simple “right answer.”

Each reading ends with **In Short**, where approximately 4–8 target expressions are bolded. These highlighted terms are intentionally connected to the Language Bank that follows.

Approximate reading bands in this edition:

- **B1:** 250–300 words
- **B2:** 300–350 words
- **C1:** 400–450 words
- **C2:** 440–500 words

These are working ranges, not hard linguistic boundaries. Cognitive demand, discourse structure, abstraction, and interactional skill are treated as more important than word count alone.

### 3. Language Bank

Each unit contains **10 high-value items** drawn from a mixture of:

- core vocabulary
- collocations
- phrasal verbs
- idioms
- natural spoken expressions
- level-appropriate technical or conceptual terms where the topic requires them

Each item contains:

1. the target word/expression
2. type
3. learner-friendly meaning
4. natural example sentence
5. a concise photorealistic image description

The goal is **usable spoken language**, not difficult vocabulary for its own sake.

### 4. Conversation Toolkit

Each unit teaches four expressions for the lesson's communication function. The language becomes progressively more sophisticated:

- B1: _I think… / The main reason is…_
- B2: _That’s a fair point, but…_
- C1: _There is an important distinction between…_
- C2: _Even if we accept that…, it does not necessarily follow that…_

This section is the functional-language spine of the curriculum.

### 5. Quick Quiz

Every unit contains **10 questions** combining:

- Language Bank recall and usage
- reading comprehension
- inference or interpretation
- natural-language usage

The cognitive demand increases by level. Advanced learners are asked to identify assumptions, tensions, qualifications, and implications rather than merely retrieve facts.

### 6. Discussion

Every lesson includes **six discussion prompts**. Questions move from accessible entry points toward deeper evaluation and conceptual challenge. They are designed to produce genuine disagreement, explanation, qualification, and follow-up—not repetitive “What do you think?” answers.

### 7. Speaking Challenge

Every lesson ends with an interactive task such as:

- decision-making
- role-play
- policy or ethics committee
- ranking
- negotiation
- case analysis
- design problem
- panel discussion
- scenario planning

The task requires learners to reuse the Conversation Toolkit and Language Bank while reaching, defending, revising, or explaining a position.

## Language Progression Principles

### Vocabulary Recycling

The intended production rule for future expansion is approximately **70% new/relevant language and 30% deliberate recycling**. Recycling can occur in readings, quiz items, discussion prompts, or speaking challenges rather than by re-teaching the same definition.

The goal is to build lexical families around recurring concepts. For example:

**limit → restriction → regulate → regulatory measure → draw the line**

Learners should gain increasingly precise ways to discuss familiar ideas as they progress.

### Idioms and Phrasal Verbs

Idioms are included only when they are natural, transferable, and useful. They are not inserted to fill a quota. Phrasal verbs and collocations are treated as core vocabulary because fluent conversation depends heavily on multi-word units.

### Grammar

Grammar is embedded in communicative purpose rather than used as unit titles. Progression emerges through increasingly complex ways of expressing condition, probability, concession, comparison, causality, and hypothetical reasoning.

## Visual Vocabulary System

Every Language Bank item includes an image description for later visual production.

### Default Style

**Photorealistic, realistic or sophisticated contemporary setting, natural lighting, authentic human expressions and body language, clean uncluttered composition, high detail, one clear action or concept, no text inside the image.**

### Visual Rules

1. **Show the meaning, not the written word.** A learner should be able to infer the basic concept from the scene.
2. **One main idea per image.** Avoid scenes containing several competing actions.
3. **Prefer human situations** for actions, emotions, phrasal verbs, idioms, and social vocabulary.
4. **Make abstract concepts concrete** through a realistic scenario rather than a decorative metaphor whenever possible.
5. **Avoid absurd literal idioms** when the literal image would teach the wrong meaning.
6. **Maintain style consistency** across the complete course.
7. **No embedded text** unless a future production workflow explicitly requires it.

### CEFR Visual Progression

- **B1:** literal everyday scenes
- **B2:** clear relationships, choices, and consequences
- **C1:** social systems and conceptual contrasts
- **C2:** nuanced situations, ambiguity, competing values, and abstract concepts represented through realistic scenarios

## Research Method

Each substantive reading was designed from a short research synthesis rather than copied from a single article. Sources include organisations and research publishers such as OECD, UNESCO, ILO, WHO, NIST, Pew Research Center, Our World in Data, the United Nations, peer-reviewed journals, the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, and relevant regulatory or institutional sources.

The writing process follows this logic:

**What is known → strongest relevant perspectives → evidence → limitations → unresolved tension → learner-appropriate synthesis**

Research Basis links are included at the end of each lesson for editorial verification and teacher reference. The learner-facing reading remains original and avoids reproducing source wording.

For contested policy or social questions, the course is designed to present relevant evidence and competing considerations without telling learners what political conclusion to adopt.

## Quality-Control Standard

A unit is considered complete only when it passes all of the following checks.

### Structure

- 7 required lesson sections
- In Short summary with highlighted target language
- 10 Language Bank entries
- a photorealistic description for every Language Bank entry
- 4 Conversation Toolkit expressions
- 10 quiz questions
- 10-answer key
- 6 discussion prompts
- final speaking challenge
- Research Basis

### Editorial Quality

- natural, idiomatic English
- CEFR-appropriate lexical and cognitive difficulty
- definitions understandable at the target level
- no unnecessary jargon
- no duplicate Language Bank items within a unit
- answer key aligned with quiz
- discussion prompts meaningfully different from each other
- factual claims qualified where evidence is uncertain or context-dependent

### Conversation Quality

A strong lesson should make it possible for two thoughtful learners to disagree for understandable reasons. The goal is not controversy for its own sake, but **productive intellectual tension**.

## Recommended Classroom Use

A full lesson can be delivered in approximately 60–90 minutes depending on learner number and speaking time. For shorter sessions, teachers can assign the reading and Language Bank before class, then use class time for the quiz review, discussion, and Speaking Challenge.

The initial Quick Vote and final speaking task can also be compared to see whether a learner's position became more nuanced after reading and discussion. Changing one's mind is not required; improving the quality of the reasoning is the goal.

---

## Expansion Path

This 40-unit version is the first curriculum spine. A later 80–100-unit edition can expand each CEFR band with additional thematic diversity, review units, speaking checkpoints, and systematic recycling.

A parallel **Business Conversation Curriculum** can reuse the same seven-part architecture while replacing general speaking challenges with realistic workplace simulations such as meetings, negotiations, client conversations, feedback, presentations, stakeholder management, and crisis communication.
