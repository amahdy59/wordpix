## 2. Read & Understand

### When “The Algorithm Did It” Is Not an Answer

Suppose an AI system denies a person a loan, recommends the wrong medical treatment, or incorrectly flags a worker as fraudulent. When harm occurs, saying “the algorithm made the decision” does not resolve responsibility. An AI system is created, trained, supplied, configured, deployed, monitored, and used by people and organisations.

This creates a chain of responsibility. Developers decide architecture and training methods. Data suppliers influence what information the system learns from. A vendor chooses what claims to make about performance. The organisation deploying the system decides where to use it, what level of risk is acceptable, and whether a human reviews the output. Individual employees may then rely on or override the result.

The OECD AI Principles state that AI actors should be accountable based on their roles, context, and ability to act, and they emphasise traceability and ongoing risk management. UNESCO similarly states that AI systems should not displace ultimate human responsibility and accountability.

That sounds clear in principle, but real systems are complex. A hospital may use software produced by one company, built partly on a model from another provider and trained on data gathered elsewhere. A harmful outcome can emerge from interactions rather than one obvious mistake.

Human oversight can also become symbolic. If a worker must approve hundreds of automated decisions quickly and lacks the information or authority to challenge them, calling that person “human in the loop” may not create meaningful control.

Accountability therefore depends on traceability: organisations need enough records to reconstruct what happened, which system version was used, what data or rules mattered, and who had authority to intervene. Independent auditing may be necessary in high-impact contexts.

Responsibility can also be divided by type. One actor may be responsible for preventing foreseeable technical failures, another for deciding whether the system is appropriate for a high-stakes setting, and another for responding when harm occurs. Separating design responsibility, deployment responsibility, and remedy can make accountability clearer than asking for one universal “owner” of every AI decision.

Remedy matters as much as blame. A person harmed by an automated decision may need an explanation, a correction, compensation, or a new decision process even when no individual acted maliciously. Accountability is therefore partly about creating routes to repair harm, not only identifying who should be punished.

The aim is not to find one person to blame for every failure. It is to prevent responsibility from falling through the cracks because each participant points to someone else. A well-governed system assigns duties before harm occurs rather than trying to invent them afterward.

### In Short

AI **accountability** requires clear **traceability**, meaningful human **oversight**, and duties across a **chain of responsibility**. In complex systems it can be difficult to **pin down** one cause, so organisations need audits and clear authority before responsibility can **fall through the cracks** or be casually **handed off** to “the algorithm.”

## 4. Conversation Toolkit

### Distributing Responsibility Precisely

**Responsibility should follow...**

> Responsibility should follow control, knowledge, and ability to prevent harm.

**That actor is responsible for..., whereas...**

> The vendor is responsible for documented system limits, whereas the hospital is responsible for appropriate deployment.

**Human oversight is only meaningful if...**

> Human oversight is only meaningful if the reviewer has time, information, and authority.

**The failure appears systemic rather than individual because...**

> The failure appears systemic because several weak controls interacted.
