## 2. Read & Understand

### When the Workday Has No Clear End

Digital communication has made work more flexible, but it has also made employees easier to reach. A message can arrive at 10 p.m., a manager can check a team chat on Sunday, and remote workers may pass their laptop every time they enter the living room. The physical boundary between “at work” and “not at work” has become easier to blur.

The “right to disconnect” generally refers to workers being able to disengage from work-related electronic communication during non-working time without negative consequences. The idea is not necessarily that no message can ever be sent after hours; rather, employees should not be expected to remain permanently available unless their role genuinely requires it.

The ILO has highlighted the importance of boundary management in telework and has discussed the ability to disconnect as one way to protect work–life balance. The concern is cumulative. A single five-minute message may seem harmless, but repeated interruptions can make personal time feel conditional and can create an always-on expectation.

A universal rule is difficult, however. Hospitals, emergency services, global teams, senior executives, and workers with flexible schedules may need different arrangements. Some employees prefer to take two hours off in the afternoon and answer messages later. A rigid “no contact after 6 p.m.” policy could reduce that flexibility.

Enforcement raises another issue. If managers officially say there is no expectation to reply but reward employees who respond immediately at night, the culture may contradict the written policy. Effective disconnection therefore depends not only on law or company rules but on workload, staffing, leadership behaviour, and performance expectations.

There is also a collective-action problem. One employee may personally choose not to answer late messages, but that choice becomes harder if colleagues who remain available gain better assignments or faster promotion. In that environment, an “individual choice” can create pressure on everyone. Organisational norms matter because workers often compare their behaviour with what seems rewarded around them.

A useful distinction is between sending and expecting. A global team may need messages to be sent across time zones while still allowing the receiver to answer during their own working hours. Delayed delivery tools, status indicators, and explicit escalation rules can protect flexibility without requiring the whole organisation to work on one clock.

The debate is ultimately about time sovereignty: how much control should workers have over the boundary between paid work and the rest of life? A workable policy has to protect rest without assuming that every profession, worker, or time zone follows the same schedule.

### In Short

A right to disconnect aims to protect the **boundary** between work and private life by reducing **after-hours** expectations and constant **availability**. Yet flexible schedules can **blur the boundaries**, and some roles require employees to **be on call**, so the challenge is to **draw a line** without creating an inflexible rule.

## 4. Conversation Toolkit

### Evaluating a Policy, Not Just the Idea

**The principle is straightforward, but implementation depends on...**

> The principle is straightforward, but implementation depends on role, schedule, and staffing.

**An unintended consequence could be...**

> An unintended consequence could be reducing flexibility for employees who prefer unusual hours.

**A policy would be ineffective if...**

> A policy would be ineffective if managers rewarded after-hours responsiveness informally.

**A workable exception would need to...**

> A workable exception would need to define genuine emergencies and compensate on-call time appropriately.
