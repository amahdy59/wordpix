## 2. Read & Understand

### Flexible Work, Uneven Risk

Digital labour platforms have changed how people find work. A driver can log on to an app, a designer can accept a project from another country, and a delivery worker can choose when to start or stop taking orders. For some workers, this flexibility is the main attraction. Platform work can provide additional income, allow people to combine work with study or family responsibilities, and connect workers with clients they would never otherwise reach.

The same flexibility can also transfer risk from the organisation to the worker. A traditional employee may receive predictable pay, paid leave, social insurance, and clearer procedures for discipline or dismissal. A platform worker may have fluctuating income, unpaid waiting time, limited social protection, and little control over changes to the algorithm that allocates work.

ILO research has repeatedly highlighted this tension. Its studies of digital labour platforms examine pay, work availability, intensity, social protection, and work–life balance. A 2026 ILO brief also emphasised how difficult it remains to measure the global number of platform workers accurately, while recent country studies continue to report both income opportunities and problems such as long hours, uncertainty, and gaps in protection.

Employment classification is central to the debate. If a platform worker is genuinely independent, the freedom to choose clients and hours may justify a different relationship from standard employment. But if the platform sets prices, controls access to customers, monitors performance, and can deactivate the worker’s account, critics ask how independent that worker really is.

Consumers and businesses are part of the system too. Customers value fast, affordable services. Platforms argue that rigid employment models could reduce flexibility or raise prices. Workers may themselves disagree: some want employee protections, while others fear losing autonomy.

Another complication is that flexibility itself has more than one form. Choosing when to log in is valuable, but it is not the same as being able to set prices, reject work without penalty, build a portable client base, or understand how ratings affect future opportunities. A worker can have schedule flexibility while remaining highly dependent on one platform for income.

Portability is another issue. Ratings, customer histories, and reputation may be valuable assets for workers, but they often remain inside one platform. If workers cannot take those records elsewhere, changing platforms can mean starting from zero, which can reduce practical independence even when formal contracts describe them as self-employed.

The gig economy therefore does not fit neatly into “good jobs” or “bad jobs.” The key question is who controls the work, who carries the risk, and whether flexibility is genuinely chosen—or simply the only available way to earn.

### In Short

Platform work can offer **flexibility**, but it can also create **precarity** when income and hours are uncertain. Debates focus on **employment classification**, **social protection**, and **algorithmic management**, especially when workers must **piece together** income while remaining **at the mercy of** platform rules.

## 4. Conversation Toolkit

### Analysing Multiple Stakeholders

**From the worker’s perspective...**

> From the worker’s perspective, flexibility may be valuable but income instability is costly.

**The platform would argue that...**

> The platform would argue that strict rules could reduce flexibility.

**For consumers, the trade-off is...**

> For consumers, the trade-off may be price versus labour protections.

**The conflict arises because...**

> The conflict arises because different stakeholders benefit from different parts of the model.

## 6. Discussion

### 1. Choice

When is flexible work genuinely a choice, and when is it a necessity?

### 2. Classification

What facts should determine whether a worker is an employee or independent contractor?

### 3. Consumers

Would you pay more for delivery if it guaranteed stronger worker protections?

### 4. Algorithms

Should workers have a right to know why an algorithm reduces their access to jobs?

### 5. Autonomy

Can someone be both highly controlled by an app and legally self-employed?

### 6. Challenge

If a worker prefers flexibility over benefits, should regulation still require minimum protections?
