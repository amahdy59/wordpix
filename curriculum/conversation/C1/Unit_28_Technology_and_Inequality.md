## 2. Read & Understand

### A Tool That Multiplies Opportunity—and Advantage

Technology can lower barriers dramatically. A student in a small town can attend an online course from another country. A small business can reach customers worldwide. Telemedicine can connect patients with specialists, and digital payments can provide services without a nearby bank branch. These examples support the idea that technology can spread opportunity.

But access is not evenly distributed. According to the International Telecommunication Union’s 2025 estimates, roughly three-quarters of the world’s population were online, leaving about 2.2 billion people offline. Coverage has expanded, yet affordability, quality, skills, gender gaps, and urban–rural differences continue to shape how much people can actually benefit.

This illustrates why the “digital divide” is not simply a line between people who have internet and people who do not. Two students may both be technically connected, while one has a private laptop, fast broadband, digital skills, and a quiet room and the other relies on a shared phone and expensive mobile data. Equal access in a narrow sense can still produce unequal outcomes.

New technologies can also reward people who already possess complementary advantages. An AI assistant may increase productivity most for people with the education to evaluate its output, reliable infrastructure, and jobs that allow them to use it. Businesses with more data and computing resources may adopt advanced systems faster than smaller competitors.

At the same time, technology can help people catch up. Translation, accessibility tools, low-cost online learning, and remote work can reduce barriers that once excluded people from information or employment.

The effect therefore depends on distribution. A technology that makes an opportunity cheaper may reduce inequality if disadvantaged groups can actually use it. The same technology may widen gaps if the benefits flow mainly to people who already have money, skills, networks, and infrastructure.

Timing matters too. New technologies are often expensive, difficult to use, or concentrated in particular organisations before they become cheaper and more widespread. An innovation can therefore widen gaps in the short term and narrow them later—or do the reverse if early advantages compound. Evaluating inequality requires asking not only whether access expands, but how quickly benefits diffuse and who captures the gains during the transition.

The relevant inequality may also change. A translation tool can reduce a language barrier while increasing the advantage of people who know how to verify its mistakes. Cheap online courses can expand access while making credentials, mentoring, and professional networks more important differentiators. Technology can close one gap and expose another.

Technological progress does not arrive on a level playing field. It changes the field—and sometimes makes existing differences more valuable.

### In Short

The **digital divide** involves not only **access** but also **affordability**, skills, and infrastructure. Technology can help disadvantaged groups **catch up** and governments can try to **bridge the gap**, but unequal resources may cause some people to be **left behind** even when everyone appears connected.

## 4. Conversation Toolkit

### Synthesising Across Different Groups

**The average effect hides differences between...**

> The average effect hides differences between connected and poorly connected communities.

**Access is necessary but not sufficient because...**

> Access is necessary but not sufficient because skills and affordability also matter.

**The same technology can have opposite effects when...**

> The same technology can reduce barriers for one group and compound advantages for another.

**The distributional question is...**

> The distributional question is who can actually convert access into opportunity.
